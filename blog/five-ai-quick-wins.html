5 AI Quick Wins to Try With Your Team.

By Jon Linton • February 3, 2026
← Back to all posts

Meeting notes, routine documents, incoming requests, internal instructions and messy spreadsheets are good places to try AI. Pick one task and measure the work from start to finish, including review.

Key Takeaways
  • Try drafting notes from one meeting. Check names, decisions and action items before sharing them.
  • Compare the time to write a routine document with the time to edit an AI draft.
  • Try sorting a sample of inquiries and check how often they reach the right person.
  • Start your knowledge base with the 10 questions new hires ask most
  • Use sample data that your team has permission to share with the tool.

Start with a task you already know well. If the tool makes a mistake, the person doing the work should be able to spot it.

1. Automate Meeting Notes and Action Items

After a meeting, someone has to turn the discussion into decisions, owners and due dates. Time that work once so you have a point of comparison.

Check whether your meeting software already includes transcription or summaries. Tell participants how the recording will be used and get any required permission before you start.

Read the draft alongside your notes. Did someone agree to send a proposal, or did the group just discuss sending one? Check the owner and the deadline before you circulate it.

2. Use AI to Draft First Versions of Routine Documents

Your company writes the same kinds of documents over and over. Proposals with the same structure. Status updates. Client emails following the same template. Standard operating procedures. Scope of work documents.

Every single one of those takes someone time to start from scratch, or they're copying and pasting from old documents and hoping they updated all the names.

Give an approved tool a sample document you have permission to reuse. Ask it to follow the structure with the details of the new job. Check names, dates, scope and prices before sending the draft.

Include editing and fact-checking when you time the trial. A draft that needs a full rewrite still counts in the comparison.

3. Set Up a Simple Customer Inquiry Triage

Right now, someone on your team probably reads every customer email, form submission, and support request by hand. They figure out what it's about. Then they route it to the right person, or they just try to handle it themselves and other things slip.

Choose a few categories from your own inbox and set a destination for each one. Give ambiguous requests a person to review them.

For example, a billing question can go to the person handling invoices. A question about a delivery date might need someone to read the whole thread before assigning it.

Review the mistakes in your sample before you turn on automatic routing.

4. Create an Internal Knowledge Base That Actually Gets Used

Walk into most companies and ask where the documentation lives. You'll hear: "It's in Google Docs. No wait, maybe Confluence. Actually, some of it is just in email threads nobody can find. And Bob probably has a lot of it in his head."

Tribal knowledge is expensive. It slows down onboarding. It means important stuff gets lost when someone leaves. It creates the constant "hey, do you know where..." Slack messages that interrupt everyone's work.

The solution: build a searchable knowledge base. Tools like Notion, Slite, or even a well-structured shared drive work. The AI part helps here because you can take messy notes, half-written documentation, and old email threads and have ChatGPT or Claude turn them into clean, searchable docs.

The tool isn't the hard part. The hard part is getting people to actually use it and contribute to it. The fix is to start small. Don't try to document everything. Start with the 10 questions new hires ask most. Document the process for your three most-repeated tasks. Get wins on the board, then expand.

When it works, you cut onboarding time, reduce interruptions, and make sure your processes don't disappear when someone quits.

5. Clean and Organize Messy Spreadsheet Data

Look for a spreadsheet with a recurring problem, such as duplicate customer records or dates entered in different formats.

Then when you actually need that data for something important, a report, an analysis, a business decision, you find out it's almost unusable.

Work on a copy. Ask the tool to flag possible duplicates and list the changes it proposes. Two customers can share a name, so check the records before merging them. Leave unknown values blank.

Use a tool and data your organization has approved for this work. A trial can use invented records with the same column layout.

Compare the cleaned sample against the original and count missed duplicates and incorrect matches.

A Quick Word on Starting Small

Pick one person to own the trial and one task they can repeat.

Check software and usage costs before expanding the trial. A useful result can be as simple as deciding which part of a task is still easier by hand.

And if you do want to go deeper, if you want to look at how AI could transform a process that's bigger than note-taking or document drafting, that's where having a partner makes sense. That's what Fresh Coast AI is here for. But you don't need to start there.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are these AI tools actually secure for business use? ▼

Check which tool, account settings and types of data your organization permits. If approval is unclear, use invented examples until the person responsible for data handling can review the setup.

What if my team doesn't want to use AI? ▼

Ask which part of the task feels like extra work. Invite someone to try it on a familiar example and decide whether the result is worth editing.

How much will this actually save me? ▼

Time the same task with and without the tool. Include setup, checking and corrections. Multiply the difference by how often you do the task and compare it with the cost of the tool.

Do I need to hire someone to manage all this? ▼

A small trial can have one owner. Agree on who checks the output, who can change the settings and where people should report a mistake.
