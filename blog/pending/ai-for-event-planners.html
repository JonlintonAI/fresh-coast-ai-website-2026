AI for Event Planners: Manage More Events Without Losing Your Mind

By Jon Linton • March 3, 2026
← Back to all posts

TL;DR: Event planning is a professional services business disguised as a creative one. Yes, the client-facing work is about vision and experience. But behind the scenes, it's proposals, vendor coordination, timelines, follow-ups, and an inbox that never stops. AI handles the coordination so your team focuses on what makes events memorable.

I was talking with an event planner in Chicago last month. Talented team, solid reputation, managing about 60 events a year across weddings, corporate conferences, and fundraising galas.

"My biggest frustration," she told me, "is that I'm drowning in coordination. The creative work—the vision, the design, the experience—that's what I love. But I'm spending 70% of my time on proposals, vendor emails, timeline management, and client check-ins. During peak season, it's suffocating."

I asked her: "How many vendors per event?"

"Fifteen to twenty," she said. "Venue, catering, florist, photographer, videographer, DJ, lighting, rentals, linens, invitations, transportation, accommodations coordination, and at least a few specialized vendors depending on the event."

"And how many of those vendors needs to be coordinated with personally?"

"All of them. Every single one. That's the job."

That's when I realized: event planning isn't a creative job that happens to have some administration. It's a coordination job that happens to have creative elements. And during peak season, managing 60+ events with 15-20 vendors each? That's the administrative equivalent of running a consulting firm—except consulting firms have deliverables. Event planners have orchestrations.

And the entire orchestration is happening in your team's heads, in email threads, in spreadsheets, and in the constant mental load of remembering what needs to happen next.

The Real Bottleneck Isn't Creativity—It's Coordination

Let me break down where the time actually goes:

Custom proposals for every event (2–3 hours each). A client inquiry comes in. You pull up a template. You customize it for their event—date, venue, guest count, event type, vendor options, pricing tiers. You adjust timelines and details. You send it. The client gives feedback. You revise. Now multiply that by 60 events a year. That's 120–180 hours of proposal work. For a small team, that's three to four full weeks of the year just writing and revising proposals.

Vendor management across 15+ vendors per event. Each vendor needs information: event details, timeline, guest count, logistics, setup requirements, special requests. And they all communicate back with questions, confirmations, and changes. You're the clearinghouse for all of it. In peak season, you might be managing communication for 60 active events × 15 vendors = 900 vendor touchpoints in the pipeline. That's not communication. That's coordination chaos.

Timeline building and constant revision. You build a master timeline for each event. Vendor setup at 2 PM. Catering prep at 3 PM. Photos at 4 PM. DJ booth at 4:30 PM. And then something changes—the photographer is running late, the florist needs to know if they can set up at 1:30, the venue has a conflicting rental. The timeline is never static. You're constantly adjusting, flagging conflicts, and communicating changes.

Client follow-ups and check-ins. Deposit received, thank you email, and here's your next step. 90 days out, let's confirm details. 30 days out, finalize vendor coordination. One week out, confirm all numbers. Two days out, final walkthrough. Post-event thank you and feedback request. That's multiple touchpoints per event, and they're all messages you're writing and sending.

Post-event wrap-up and thank-yous. After the event, you send thank-you notes to vendors. You collect photos and testimonials. You request reviews. You archive all the details for future reference. Another 2–3 hours per event. That's 120–180 hours per year if you have 60 events.

Your software (HoneyBook, Dubsado, Aisle Planner) gives you structure. But structure isn't thinking. You still have to do the thinking.

Where AI Changes the Equation

Here's what becomes possible when you bring AI into the coordination workflow:

Proposal generation. A client fills out your intake form. Venue, date, guest count, event type, special requests. AI generates a first draft of the proposal—automatically pulling in your standard options, pricing tiers, timeline templates, and vendor suggestions relevant to that event type. Your team reviews it, tweaks the creative elements, adjusts pricing based on complexity, and sends it. You've gone from "start with a blank template and write 2–3 hours" to "review an intelligent draft and refine for 30 minutes." That's 1–2 hours saved per proposal. For 60 events, that's a full month recovered.

Vendor communication. AI drafts routine vendor updates, availability checks, and confirmations. "Hi, florist team—just confirming your 2 PM setup time for the Johnson wedding on May 15th. Guest count is locked at 120. Any questions?" Instead of your coordinator writing 15 of these emails per event, they review the drafts, hit send, and move on. You're not replacing judgment; you're eliminating the typing.

Timeline management. AI builds initial timelines based on your templates and past events. Then it flags potential conflicts. "DJ booth setup at 4:30 PM conflicts with photographer transition. Florist needs to confirm they can finish by 4:15 PM." Your team doesn't have to manually scan 50 timeline items looking for overlaps. The AI finds them. Your team adjusts the details.

Client follow-up sequences. Instead of your coordinator manually writing check-in emails at key milestones, AI sends them automatically—but personalized, not robotic. Deposit received: "Thanks so much. Here's what happens next." 90 days out: "Let's confirm your vision. Here are the details we have locked in." 30 days out: "Vendor coordination is underway. Any final tweaks?" These emails feel personal because they reference actual event details. But the writing and scheduling is automated.

None of this replaces judgment. None of it removes your team from decision-making. But it removes the busywork that's currently burying them.

Why "Build Together" Matters for Event Planners

Here's the critical part: your coordinators' judgment and creativity are the product. They know what questions to ask. They know which vendors work well together. They notice when a timeline is getting too tight or when a client's expectations don't match the budget. They make the calls that keep events running smoothly.

AI needs to amplify that judgment, not replace it.

If your coordinators feel like AI is a tool they're using—"I can generate proposals 10x faster, so I have time to actually talk to clients about their vision"—they'll use it constantly. If they feel like AI is taking over—"I'm just approving what the robot generated"—the tool will gather dust and breed resentment.

The difference between a platform that saves your peak season and one that creates friction is whether your team feels like owners of the process or like they're being replaced by it.

So if you're thinking about bringing AI into your event planning business, the first question isn't "What can AI do?" It's "What do my coordinators hate doing the most?" Find the task that's driving them crazy—probably vendor coordination emails—and start there. Let them experience what it feels like to have that weight lifted. Once they see that, adoption becomes natural.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will my events feel less personal? ▼

No. AI is handling the administrative coordination—the emails, timelines, vendor checklists. Your team is still making all the creative decisions and client-facing choices. In fact, because your coordinators aren't buried in logistics, they have more mental space for the details that make events memorable.

Can AI handle the variety in event types? ▼

Yes. You train AI on your proposal templates, your typical timelines, your vendor lists, and your standard processes. Then it adapts those templates to different event types—corporate conference, wedding, product launch, nonprofit gala. The variations exist, but the core coordination logic is the same.

What about my existing planning software? ▼

Most modern event planning platforms (HoneyBook, Dubsado, Aisle Planner) have some level of integration or API access. AI tools can pull data from your system, use it to generate proposals and timelines, and push updates back. If you're using something with limited integration, AI can still work with manual updates—it's just less seamless.
